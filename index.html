What to look for

Chorea
Brief movements of about 0.15–0.7 s that flow from one joint to the next. They are most frequent in the fingers, wrists, toes and ankles, with intermittent elbow and knee involvement and shoulder shrugs.
Ballismus
Sudden, large flings that start at the shoulder or hip. Onset is fast and the return is slower, with a passive whip of the wrist and fingers.
Not tremor
Event timing is random (Poisson), so intervals never settle into a frequency. Watch the strip chart: there is no periodicity.
Not dystonia
Every excursion returns toward rest. No posture is held.
Not myoclonus
Myoclonic jerks are shock-like and usually under 100 ms. These movements are longer and smoother.
Not a seizure
The head stays upright and oriented, the limbs move out of sync, and nothing evolves through tonic and clonic phases.
Not voluntary
There is no goal or rhythm. Postural corrections lag the movement and are not anticipatory. A reactive step sometimes follows a large fling.

Clinical correlation

  • Laterality. Most reported cases are unilateral (hemichorea–hemiballismus); bilateral involvement is less common.
  • Imaging. CT hyperdensity and T1 hyperintensity of the putamen, with or without the caudate, lie contralateral to the affected limbs. Left-greater-than-right movements predict right-greater-than-left striatal signal.
  • Course. Movements often improve as glucose is corrected. Some patients need symptomatic therapy when movements persist.
  • Rehabilitation. The main risks are falls from leg flings and reactive steps, and limb injury from striking bed rails or furniture. Plan for padding, supervised mobility and a safe environment.
